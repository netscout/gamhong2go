## Section 0: Background -- What Is a Language Card?

### What It Is

The Apple II+ shipped in 1979 with Applesoft BASIC burned into the firmware ROM at $D000-$FFFF. That is 12 KB of non-volatile storage that the CPU reads at every boot, and that ordinary software cannot touch. For most users, that ROM was perfect: it held a polished BASIC interpreter, the Monitor debugger, and Wozniak's floating-point library.

For serious developers, it was a wall. CP/M required an 8080 emulator that needed to live somewhere in the upper address space. Pascal and Forth systems wanted custom firmware. Integer BASIC fans (who disliked Applesoft) wanted to swap the interpreter. All of those use cases needed writable memory at $D000-$FFFF -- but the hardware wired that range straight to ROM chips.

Apple's answer was the **Language Card**: a small expansion board that plugged into slot 0 (the leftmost expansion slot, closest to the power supply). The card added 16 KB of RAM and a bank-switching circuit wired to a 16-address control window at $C080-$C08F. Software could flip these softswitches to redirect the CPU's $D000-$FFFF reads and writes away from the firmware ROM and into the card's RAM.

Later Apple II+ machines shipped with the language card already installed on the motherboard. It is unconditionally present in this emulator.

**Why 16 KB of RAM for only 12 KB of address space?** The address window $D000-$FFFF is 12 KB, but the card holds 16 KB. The extra 4 KB is a second bank that shares the lower 4 KB of the window. $D000-$DFFF can be mapped to either bank 1 or bank 2 -- one at a time -- while $E000-$FFFF always maps to a single shared 8 KB region. This lets a developer load two different 4 KB firmware images (for instance, Integer BASIC and a debugging toolkit) and switch between them by toggling one bit.

**Scope boundary**: This walkthrough covers the Apple II+ language card. The Apple IIe added a richer "extended 80-column card" with a different softswitch layout at $C00x and an auxiliary-memory select -- that is a different design and is outside scope. The $C011/$C012/$C016 status-read registers (which let software query the current LC mode without changing it) are named here for reader awareness but are not wired in `io.SoftSwitches` -- out of scope. The Disk II controller has read-side-effect softswitches at $C0E0-$C0EF that superficially resemble $C08x but have nothing to do with bank switching -- see `15.walkthrough_disk_controller.md` §3 (strobe dispatch) and §5 (Q6/Q7 four-mode matrix).

**Power-on state**: the card resets to ROM-read mode, write-disabled, bank 2 selected. Software that never touches $C080-$C08F sees the machine behave exactly as if no language card were present. That is deliberate -- the firmware at $D000-$FFFF boots without any knowledge of the LC, and the LC must stay invisible until explicitly activated.

For the bus routing context, see the six-device diagram in `04.walkthrough_bus.md` Section 0, which shows the LC as the last-mapped device at $D000-$FFFF and at $C080-$C08F. The $D000-$FFFF region is also referenced in the "strictly speaking" caveat at `03.walkthrough_memory.md` Section 0 -- that caveat describes exactly what this walkthrough documents. Note that the video system explicitly does not read from LC RAM ($D000-$FFFF) -- see the LC caveat in `08.walkthrough_video.md` Section 0.

### Why It Matters

The language card is what made the Apple II viable as a serious development machine in the early 1980s. CP/M capability (via a Z80 plug-in card that needed the LC's RAM), Pascal systems, Forth interpreters, and assembly-language monitors all depended on writable $D000-$FFFF. Without the LC, those systems could not run. In the emulator, the LC is also the first example of a device that both overlays another device and delegates back to it -- the bus-layering pattern it establishes reappears in the disk controller.

### How the Code Works

Two files implement the language card:

- `languageCard/card.go` (153 LOC): the `Card` struct, constructor, reset, read/write routing, RAM backing store, and the `handleSwitch` state machine.
- `languageCard/softswitches.go` (26 LOC): the thin `Switches` adapter that exposes `Card.handleSwitch` to the bus at $C080-$C08F.

### Real-World Analogy

Imagine a car that comes from the factory with a read-only navigation computer behind the dashboard. You cannot reprogram the maps -- they are burned into the chip. A "language card" is like a slot under the dashboard into which you slide a programmable cartridge. Flip one lever ($C08x) and the dashboard reads from the cartridge instead of the factory chip. Flip it back and the factory chip is in control again. The cartridge has two trays in its lower compartment (bank 1 and bank 2), so you can keep two different map sets loaded and toggle between them. The steering wheel, the pedals, and the engine (the CPU and the bus) do not change; only which memory the dashboard reads.

## Section 1: Geometry -- 4 KB + 4 KB + 8 KB = 16 KB over 12 KB of Address Space

### What It Is

The language card holds 16 KB of RAM spread across three slices, which back a 12 KB address window in the CPU's address space. Understanding why the card holds more RAM than the window it covers is the key to understanding the entire design.

`languageCard/card.go:16-18` declares the three backing slices:

```go
bank1 [0x1000]uint8 // $D000-$DFFF bank 1 (4 KB)
bank2 [0x1000]uint8 // $D000-$DFFF bank 2 (4 KB)
upper [0x2000]uint8 // $E000-$FFFF shared (8 KB)
```

### Why It Matters

The 4 KB address window $D000-$DFFF can be occupied by either `bank1` or `bank2` -- never both simultaneously. The `bank2Sel` flag picks which one is active. This gives software two separate 4 KB scratchpads that share one address window: load Image A into bank 1, load Image B into bank 2, and toggle `bank2Sel` to switch between them without transferring any bytes across the bus.

$E000-$FFFF has no companion bank. Any access to an address above $DFFF always hits `upper`, regardless of `bank2Sel`.

**Diagram 1: Bank-switching geometry**

```text
  Card RAM (16 KB total)                CPU address window (12 KB)
  +---------------------+               +---------------------+
  |  bank1  [4 KB]      |---+           | $D000               |
  |  ($D000-$DFFF)      |   |           |   selected bank     |
  +---------------------+   +---(mux)-->|   (bank1 OR bank2)  |
  |  bank2  [4 KB]      |   |           |                     |
  |  ($D000-$DFFF)      |---+           | $DFFF               |
  +---------------------+               +---------------------+
  |  upper  [8 KB]      |------(wire)-->| $E000               |
  |  ($E000-$FFFF)      |               |   shared 8 KB       |
  +---------------------+               | $FFFF               |
                                        +---------------------+
```

### How the Code Works

`languageCard/card.go:6-10` documents the mux in the type comment:

```
// Memory layout: 4 KB bank1 + 4 KB bank2 (both map to $D000-$DFFF;
// one selected at a time) + 8 KB shared for $E000-$FFFF.
// Total 16 KB. Bank1 and bank2 share the same address window, which
// is why the card holds 16 KB of RAM overlaying only 12 KB of address
// space.
```

`languageCard/card.go:12-15` explains the three-slice choice:

```
// RAM backing store. Kept as three distinct slices rather than a
// single 16 KB buffer so the hot path stays branch-light: the
// read/write selector only picks bank1 vs bank2 for $D000-$DFFF;
// $E000-$FFFF is a direct slice.
```

A single 16 KB buffer would require a two-step decode on every access: first subtract the base address, then check whether the result falls in the lower 4 KB, and if so, add another offset for whichever bank is active. Three slices mean the hot path peeks `addr >= 0xE000` once; if false, it picks `bank1` or `bank2` directly. One branch fewer on the critical read/write path.

Bank 1 and bank 2 are never active simultaneously. Software that wants to move data between banks must shuttle bytes through the other bank by temporarily switching `bank2Sel`.

### Real-World Analogy

Think of two interchangeable 4 KB drawers stacked behind the same cutout in a wall, plus a single 8 KB drawer above them. Only one of the two lower drawers faces outward at a time; the upper drawer is always the same drawer. No matter which lower drawer you pull out, its items appear at the same addresses from the outside. You cannot open both lower drawers at once.

## Section 2: The `Card` Struct -- Four Bits of State and a ROM Pointer

### What It Is

`languageCard/card.go:11-33` defines the `Card` struct: three RAM backing arrays, four mode bits, and one interface pointer.

The four mode bits drive every routing decision:

| Field      | Type | Meaning                                                      |
|------------|------|--------------------------------------------------------------|
| `ramRead`  | bool | true = reads at $D000-$FFFF return LC RAM; false = ROM       |
| `ramWrite` | bool | true = writes at $D000-$FFFF land in LC RAM; false = dropped |
| `bank2Sel` | bool | **true** = bank 2 active at $D000-$DFFF; false = bank 1     |
| `prearm`   | bool | internal flip-flop for the two-read write-enable quirk       |

The fifth member, `rom ROMReader`, is a direct pointer to the ROM device the card overlays.

### Why It Matters

`ramRead` and `ramWrite` are independent. Software can enable reads from LC RAM while leaving writes disabled (useful for verifying what is in RAM without risk of corruption), or enable writes without enabling reads (useful for initializing RAM while the CPU still executes from ROM). The card can also be in full ROM-mode (both false) or full RAM-mode (both true).

`bank2Sel` is a field name that reads slightly backwards: `bank2Sel = true` means bank 2 is selected; `bank2Sel = false` means bank 1 is selected. This inversion from what a reader might intuit (one might expect a field named `bank1Sel`) is worth noting explicitly. Every place in the code where bank selection matters, the condition is `bank2Sel == true` for bank 2 and `!bank2Sel` (or `default`) for bank 1.

### How the Code Works

`languageCard/card.go:37-39` defines `ROMReader`:

```go
type ROMReader interface {
    Read(addr uint16) uint8
}
```

One method. The card only needs to read the ROM -- never write it, never ask its size. This is the minimal-interface principle in action: expose only what you need, nothing more. `memory.ROM` satisfies this interface (see `03.walkthrough_memory.md` Section 3 for the `ROM.Read` implementation).

The `ROMReader` pointer deserves careful attention. `languageCard/card.go:30-32` explains the design constraint:

```
// Keeping a direct pointer (vs re-querying the bus) avoids recursion
// hazards -- the bus maps the LC at the same range the ROM
// originally held, and the LC owns the fallthrough.
```

Expanding on that comment:

> The card owns the fallthrough decision. When `ramRead` is false, the
> CPU's read at, say, $D123 still lands on the *card*, not on the ROM
> -- the bus routed it to the LC because the LC is the last-mapped
> device covering $D000-$FFFF. The card then chooses to delegate to
> the ROM by calling `c.rom.Read(addr)`. The bus is never consulted a
> second time. If it were, the bus would hand the read right back to
> the LC (last-mapping-wins), and we would be in an infinite loop
> before the first instruction finished. The direct `ROMReader`
> pointer is what prevents that.

See `04.walkthrough_bus.md` Section 1 (Device contract) and Section 4 (last-mapping-wins) for the bus-level mechanics that make this necessary.

### Real-World Analogy

Think of the card as a bilingual switchboard operator. Every call (memory access) to extension $D000-$FFFF comes to the operator -- because the operator is the last one registered to that extension range. If the operator's `ramRead` light is on, they handle the call themselves (LC RAM). If it is off, they forward the call directly to the firmware department (`c.rom.Read`). They never ask the switchboard to transfer the call -- that would just route it back to themselves.

## Section 3: `NewCard` and `Reset` -- Power-On Defaults

### What It Is

`languageCard/card.go:44-48` constructs the card:

```go
func NewCard(rom ROMReader) *Card {
    c := &Card{rom: rom}
    c.Reset()
    return c
}
```

`languageCard/card.go:52-57` resets the mode bits to Apple II+ power-on state:

```go
func (c *Card) Reset() {
    c.ramRead = false
    c.ramWrite = false
    c.bank2Sel = true
    c.prearm = false
}
```

### Why It Matters

Four things follow from this power-on state:

1. `ramRead=false` -- every read at $D000-$FFFF falls through to `c.rom.Read`. The LC is invisible to the reset handler; the CPU boots as if no card were present.
2. `ramWrite=false` -- every write at $D000-$FFFF is silently dropped. The firmware ROM cannot be accidentally overwritten by stray writes.
3. `bank2Sel=true` -- bank 2 is selected by default. This is the historical Apple II+ hardware convention (the board's default jumper wiring). When software later flips `ramRead` on without explicitly picking a bank, it gets bank 2. Software that cares specifies the bank explicitly via a READBSR1 or READBSR2 strobe.
4. `prearm=false` -- the write-enable flip-flop (see Section 4.3) starts unset, so a single inadvertent read of a WRITEBSR address cannot enable writes.

**Reset does not clear RAM.** `languageCard/card.go:50-51` states this explicitly:

```
// Reset returns the card to Apple II+ power-on state. Does NOT clear
// RAM contents (matches hardware: RAM retains across reset).
```

On real Apple II+ hardware, the reset line is a signal line wired to the CPU and some peripherals; it does not touch DRAM. The RAM on the language card holds its contents through a reset. Some software (warm-boot detectors, crash-recovery routines) counted on this: they stored a sentinel byte in LC RAM before invoking a reset and checked for it on the next boot to distinguish a cold start from a warm restart. Clearing RAM in `Reset()` would break those programs. The emulator faithfully matches this behavior.

`rom` must be non-nil. The card has no defensive nil-check in its fallthrough path; if a caller passes nil, the first LC read while `ramRead=false` will panic on `c.rom.Read` dispatch. That is the documented constructor contract -- see the comparison with `memory.ROM` construction in `03.walkthrough_memory.md` Section 2.

### How the Code Works

`NewCard` delegates to `Reset()` rather than initializing the mode bits inline. This ensures the constructor and the reset path always produce identical starting state -- a pattern that also appears in `memory.RAM` and other devices throughout the codebase.

### Real-World Analogy

A hardware reset on the Apple II is like pulling the emergency stop cord on a train. The train halts (CPU stops executing), but the cargo in the freight car (LC RAM) is not unloaded. When the conductor releases the brake (CPU restarts from the reset vector), the cargo is still there exactly as left. Only the train's controls (the mode bits) are reset to their safe default positions.

## Section 4: The $C080-$C08F Softswitches -- Truth Table and the `handleSwitch` Function

### What It Is

Sixteen addresses, $C080 through $C08F, form the language card's control window. A CPU read or write to any address in this range is side-effect-only: the byte the CPU writes is ignored, and the byte a read returns is always 0 (a known divergence from real hardware -- see Section 6). The address itself -- specifically bits 3, 1, and 0 of its low nibble -- carries the command.

`languageCard/softswitches.go:16-19` handles reads and `:24-26` handles writes. Both extract `addr & 0x0F` and forward it to `Card.handleSwitch` (`languageCard/card.go:109-153`).

### Why It Matters

These 16 addresses are the sole interface between software and the language card. Every LC feature -- bank select, RAM read, RAM write, the two-read quirk -- flows through `handleSwitch`. A reader who understands this function can predict the complete state of the card after any sequence of accesses.

### How the Code Works

#### Section 4.1 -- The Address Decode

The low nibble of the address (bits 3..0) is the only thing that matters. The high bits $C08_ merely name the window. Within the low nibble, three independent signal groups exist:

- **bit 3** (`low & 0x08`) -- bank select. **Clear (0) means bank 2; set (1) means bank 1.** This is the inversion that trips readers most often: the bit that selects bank 1 is *set*, not clear. `languageCard/card.go:111` decodes this as `c.bank2Sel = (low & 0x08) == 0`. When bit 3 is 0, the expression is true, so `bank2Sel = true` (bank 2 selected). When bit 3 is 1, the expression is false, so `bank2Sel = false` (bank 1 selected). **Bit 3 is the bank-select bit -- not bit 1 as a reader might guess from the address ordering.** That misconception is the single most common trap in the entire package.

- **bit 2** (`low & 0x04`) -- a mirror/alias bit with no behavioral effect. Addresses $C084-$C087 behave identically to $C080-$C083, and $C08C-$C08F to $C088-$C08B. The Apple II+ hardware address decoder examines bits 0-1 and bit 3 only. The code mirrors this: the switch statement tests `low & 0x03`, which silently discards bit 2. That discarding is the mirror behavior.

- **bits 1..0** (`low & 0x03`) -- action selector. Four values map to four behaviors:

**Diagram 2: $C08x truth table**

```text
  $C08x  low nibble  bank  ramRead  write-behavior
  -----  ----------  ----  -------  ---------------
  $C080     0x0       2     RAM      disable (READBSR2)
  $C081     0x1       2     ROM      arm/commit (WRITEBSR2)
  $C082     0x2       2     ROM      disable (OFFBSR2)
  $C083     0x3       2     RAM      arm/commit (RDWRBSR2)
  $C084     0x4       2     RAM      disable (mirror of $C080)
  $C085     0x5       2     ROM      arm/commit (mirror of $C081)
  $C086     0x6       2     ROM      disable (mirror of $C082)
  $C087     0x7       2     RAM      arm/commit (mirror of $C083)
  $C088     0x8       1     RAM      disable (READBSR1)
  $C089     0x9       1     ROM      arm/commit (WRITEBSR1)
  $C08A     0xA       1     ROM      disable (OFFBSR1)
  $C08B     0xB       1     RAM      arm/commit (RDWRBSR1)
  $C08C     0xC       1     RAM      disable (mirror of $C088)
  $C08D     0xD       1     ROM      arm/commit (mirror of $C089)
  $C08E     0xE       1     ROM      disable (mirror of $C08A)
  $C08F     0xF       1     RAM      arm/commit (mirror of $C08B)
```

The `BSR1`/`BSR2` mnemonics (Bank-Switch RAM, bank 1/bank 2) are the Apple II community's historical names. They appear in virtually every other Language Card document you will encounter.

**Writes to $C08x are simpler than reads.** When the CPU writes to any address in this range (an `STA $C08x` instruction), bank-select and `ramRead` still update per the same decode, but: **no write ever enables `ramWrite`**, and **every write unconditionally clears `prearm`**. The relevant code paths are `languageCard/card.go:121-125` (case 1) and `languageCard/card.go:142-144` (case 3). Reads can arm and commit the write-enable flip-flop; writes to the control window cannot. This asymmetry is the hardware protection mechanism described in Section 4.3.

#### Section 4.2 -- Decoding `handleSwitch` Line by Line

`languageCard/card.go:109-153` is the complete state machine. Reading it top to bottom:

1. `languageCard/card.go:111` -- `c.bank2Sel = (low & 0x08) == 0`. Bank select is applied unconditionally on every access, before the action switch. Both reads and writes update the bank select. A CPU write to $C081 (WRITEBSR2) updates the bank to 2 and then goes through case 1's write path -- bank select is never skipped.

2. `languageCard/card.go:113-152` -- `switch low & 0x03`. Four cases:

   - **Case 0** (READBSR): `ramRead=true`, `ramWrite=false`, `prearm=false`. The "quiet" read-enable switch. Sets RAM reading on, kills write-enable, kills prearm. Unconditional -- does not check `isWrite`.

   - **Case 2** (OFFBSR): `ramRead=false`, `ramWrite=false`, `prearm=false`. The "full off" switch. Puts the card back into ROM-read mode with writes disabled. Also unconditional.

   - **Case 1** (WRITEBSR): `ramRead=false`. Then branches on `isWrite`. If `isWrite=true` (CPU wrote to the address): clear both `prearm` and `ramWrite`. If `isWrite=false` (CPU read): step the arm/commit dance (Section 4.3).

   - **Case 3** (RDWRBSR): identical to case 1 except `ramRead=true` instead of `false`.

Cases 0 and 2 are the "quiet" switches -- they never arm anything, never set `ramWrite`, and always clear both `prearm` and `ramWrite`. Cases 1 and 3 are the "busy" switches that drive the two-read dance. Note also that cases 1 and 3 differ only in their `ramRead` assignment: WRITEBSR gives ROM reads + potential write-enable; RDWRBSR gives RAM reads + the same potential write-enable.

#### Section 4.3 -- The Two-Consecutive-Reads Write-Enable Quirk

This is the conceptually hardest part of the language card design.

> On a real Apple Language Card, the write-enable line is not
> controlled by a single address strobe. To *enable* writes to LC
> RAM, the CPU must read the same WRITEBSR or RDWRBSR address (or
> one with the same bits 0..1) **twice in a row**, with no
> intervening disable-write switch in between. The first read
> "arms" an internal flip-flop; the second read "commits" it and
> actually flips the ramWrite line. A write to any $C08x address
> clears the arm flip-flop immediately -- the CPU cannot commit by
> writing. Any read of a disable-write address (case 0 or 2) also
> clears the arm.
>
> The purpose of this quirk is to prevent accidental RAM writes.
> A stray `STA abs` into the $C08x range would flip the softswitch
> but could not enable writes; ordinary BASIC or assembler code
> that merely *reads* one of these addresses once (for example to
> query the current mode) could not enable writes either. Only
> deliberate two-read sequences -- a pattern no unintentional code
> path produces -- arm *and* commit the write line.
>
> In the emulator this flip-flop is the `prearm` bool in the Card
> struct. It is *not* the same as `ramWrite`. `prearm` means "one
> more arm-read will flip ramWrite on." `ramWrite` is the actual
> routing decision used in `Write()`.

The relevant code paths are `languageCard/card.go:119-132` (case 1, WRITEBSR) and `languageCard/card.go:139-151` (case 3, RDWRBSR). The write-access short-circuit at `languageCard/card.go:121-125` shows the fence: `if isWrite { c.ramWrite = false; c.prearm = false }`.

**Diagram 3: Two-reads write-enable state machine**

```text
   power-on / Reset
         |
         v
  +---------------------+
  |  prearm  = false    |  <---------------+
  |  ramWrite = false   |                  |
  +---------------------+                  |
         |                                 |
         | first read of WRITEBSR or       |
         | RDWRBSR (case 1 or case 3,      |
         | isWrite=false)                  |
         v                                 |
  +---------------------+                  |
  |  prearm  = true     |                  |
  |  ramWrite = false   |                  |
  +---------------------+                  |
         |                                 |
         | second read of same-class       |
         | arm address (low & 3 matches)   |
         v                                 |
  +---------------------+                  |
  |  prearm  = true     | -----------------+
  |  ramWrite = true    |
  +---------------------+
   any disable-write edge
   (case 0 or case 2 read,
    OR any WRITE to $C08x)
    returns to top box
```

In the committed state (`prearm=true`, `ramWrite=true`), further reads of case-1 or case-3 addresses are idempotent -- `ramWrite` stays true, `prearm` stays true. Only a disable-write edge (case 0, case 2, or any $C08x write) returns the machine to the initial state.

Additional notes on the mechanics:

- Two reads of **different** arm addresses still work, as long as both share the same `low & 0x03` value. $C081 followed by $C085 (both have `low & 0x03 == 1`) still commits. The code only checks bits 0..1. Bit 3 (bank select) is applied on every access, so two reads $C081 then $C089 commit `ramWrite` *and* flip the bank between the two reads -- this is correct Apple II+ behavior.
- Any write to any $C08x address breaks the arm chain. Cases 0 and 2 clear `prearm` unconditionally regardless of `isWrite`; cases 1 and 3 take the `if isWrite` short-circuit at `card.go:121-125` and `card.go:142-144`. Both paths converge on `prearm = false`.
- `Reset()` also clears `prearm` -- `languageCard/card.go:56`.

### Real-World Analogy

A two-key missile-silo launch panel. One key turns a safety interlock (`prearm`); a second turn of the same interlock confirms the intent and closes the launch circuit (`ramWrite=true`). Pulling either key out (any write to $C08x, or a read of a disable-write switch) resets both keys to the safe position. A single stray keypress cannot launch -- only a deliberate sequence of two consecutive turns by someone who knows the protocol.

## Section 5: `Read` and `Write` on the LC -- The ROM-vs-RAM Mux

### What It Is

With the four mode bits established, `Card.Read` and `Card.Write` implement the per-access routing decision. Both are at `languageCard/card.go:61-82`; the shared address decoder for the read path is `ramSlot` at `languageCard/card.go:85-94`.

### Why It Matters

These three functions are the observable surface of the language card. Every memory access the CPU makes to $D000-$FFFF passes through one of them. The mode bits set by `handleSwitch` determine which backing store serves each access.

### How the Code Works

`languageCard/card.go:61-66` -- `Card.Read`:

```go
func (c *Card) Read(addr uint16) uint8 {
    if c.ramRead {
        return c.ramSlot(addr)
    }
    return c.rom.Read(addr)
}
```

One question: is `ramRead` true? If yes, call `ramSlot`; if no, delegate to `c.rom.Read`. The ROM delegation produces the reset-state-looks-like-no-LC behavior described in Section 0 and in `03.walkthrough_memory.md` Section 0. No bus involvement -- the delegation is a direct method call on the ROM device.

`languageCard/card.go:70-82` -- `Card.Write`:

```go
func (c *Card) Write(addr uint16, val uint8) {
    if !c.ramWrite {
        return
    }
    switch {
    case addr >= 0xE000:
        c.upper[addr-0xE000] = val
    case c.bank2Sel:
        c.bank2[addr-0xD000] = val
    default:
        c.bank1[addr-0xD000] = val
    }
}
```

One question: is `ramWrite` true? If no, the write evaporates silently -- the same empty-body write behavior as `memory.ROM.Write` (see `03.walkthrough_memory.md` Section 3). If yes, the address routes to the correct slice.

`languageCard/card.go:85-94` -- `ramSlot`: the read-side address decoder.

```go
func (c *Card) ramSlot(addr uint16) uint8 {
    switch {
    case addr >= 0xE000: // $E000-$FFFF shared
        return c.upper[addr-0xE000]
    case c.bank2Sel:
        return c.bank2[addr-0xD000]
    default:
        return c.bank1[addr-0xD000]
    }
}
```

`ramSlot` is a textbook single-responsibility factoring: one function owns the "which slice for this address" decision, and `Read` delegates to it. `Write` inlines the same switch rather than calling `ramSlot` because `ramSlot` returns a byte by value, not a pointer to a slice element. Sharing the decode would require factoring out a separate `ramSlotPtr(addr) *uint8` helper; at 6 lines of inline switch, that extra abstraction is not worth it.

**Diagram 4: LC read and write paths**

```text
  CPU: Read($D500)            CPU: Read($E500)          CPU: Write($D500, $42)
       |                           |                         |
       v                           v                         v
  bus (last-match) -> LC.Read                          bus -> LC.Write
       |                                                    |
       v                                                    v
  ramRead?                                            ramWrite?
    true  --> ramSlot($D500)                            false -> return (drop)
               |                                        true  --> switch addr
               v                                                     |
          addr >= $E000? -> upper[addr-$E000]                        v
          bank2Sel?      -> bank2[addr-$D000]               addr >= $E000?
          else           -> bank1[addr-$D000]               -> upper[addr-$E000]
    false --> c.rom.Read($D500)                          bank2Sel?
               (ROM device, no bus re-entry)             -> bank2[addr-$D000]
                                                         else
                                                         -> bank1[addr-$D000]
```

### Real-World Analogy

The card is a librarian sitting in front of two filing cabinets (ROM and LC RAM). Every request ($D000-$FFFF) arrives at the librarian's desk. If the `ramRead` sign on the desk is lit, the librarian reaches into the LC RAM cabinets. If it is dark, the librarian passes the request back to the original archive (ROM). Write requests work the same way: `ramWrite` lit means the librarian files the new page into the LC RAM cabinets; `ramWrite` dark means the page goes into the shredder.

## Section 6: The `Switches` Shim -- Address-Strobed, Not Data-Latched

### What It Is

`languageCard/softswitches.go:7-10` defines the adapter that connects `Card.handleSwitch` to the bus at $C080-$C08F:

```go
type Switches struct{ c *Card }

func NewSwitches(c *Card) *Switches { return &Switches{c: c} }
```

### Why It Matters

`Card` is mapped at $D000-$FFFF. The language card also needs to be reachable at $C080-$C08F -- a completely different address region. A single Go value cannot have two `Read` methods with different behaviors for different address ranges. The `Switches` shim is the solution: a separate type that holds a pointer to the `Card` and exposes `bus.Device` methods that forward only to `handleSwitch`.

This is the same pattern used throughout the emulator when one logical device needs to appear at multiple disjoint bus windows.

### How the Code Works

`languageCard/softswitches.go:16-19` -- `Read`:

```go
func (s *Switches) Read(addr uint16) uint8 {
    s.c.handleSwitch(uint8(addr&0x0F), false /*isWrite*/)
    return 0
}
```

Side effect: `handleSwitch` runs. Return value: always 0.

`languageCard/softswitches.go:24-26` -- `Write`:

```go
func (s *Switches) Write(addr uint16, _ uint8) {
    s.c.handleSwitch(uint8(addr&0x0F), true /*isWrite*/)
}
```

The value parameter is named `_` -- explicitly discarded. The Apple II+ softswitches are **address-strobed, not data-latched**: the CPU's `STA $C080` cycle raises an address-decode signal that the flip-flop sees; the byte the CPU happens to place on the data bus during that cycle is irrelevant to the switch. See `05.walkthrough_io.md` for the general I/O softswitch pattern -- the LC softswitches are a specialized instance of the same idea. Writes flip the bank select and clear prearm; they never enable `ramWrite`. The distinction matters because a programmer accustomed to I2C or SPI registers might expect the written value to matter.

**Return value of Read is 0**: on real Apple II+ hardware, reading $C08x returns the "floating-bus byte" -- the most recent byte the video DMA circuitry put on the data bus, which depends on the exact cycle timing of the video scan. `languageCard/softswitches.go:14-15` documents this divergence:

```
// Returns 0. Real II+ hardware returns the floating-bus byte (most
// recent video-scan byte); returning 0 is a known divergence
```

Emulating floating-bus behavior requires a cycle-accurate video clock that the current I/O layer does not carry. Returning 0 is consistent with how `03.walkthrough_memory.md` Section 3 handles out-of-range ROM reads (returns 0xFF as a harmless constant rather than modeling the true open-bus state).

**Install ordering**: `languageCard/softswitches.go:4-6` states that the `Switches` device must be mapped **after** `io.SoftSwitches`. The bus's last-mapping-wins rule (see `04.walkthrough_bus.md` Section 4) means the last device registered for a given address range wins the decode. `io.SoftSwitches` is mapped at $C000-$C0FF, which includes $C080-$C08F. If `Switches` is mapped afterward at $C080-$C08F, it wins the overlap for those 16 addresses. The authoritative install sequence is in `main.go:97, 112`.

### Real-World Analogy

Think of the `Switches` shim as a dedicated reception phone for a building's side entrance. The main entrance (`Card` mapped at $D000-$FFFF) already has its own phone line. The side entrance ($C080-$C08F softswitches) needs to reach the same building interior (`handleSwitch`) without going through the front desk. The `Switches` shim is that dedicated line -- when the bus places a call to $C080-$C08F, it rings straight through to `handleSwitch`. Whether anything was said (the written byte) is irrelevant; the call being placed is the entire signal.

## Section 7: Putting It Together -- End-to-End Install and Boot Trace

### Install Order

The install sequence in `main.go:96-112` establishes three layers:

```go
b.Map(0x0000, 0xBFFF, ram)          // line 96: RAM  $0000-$BFFF
b.Map(0xC000, 0xC0FF, sw)           // line 97: I/O  $C000-$C0FF
b.Map(rom.Base, rom.End(), rom)     // line 98: ROM  $D000-$FFFF (covers $D000-$FFFF for all three shipped ROMs; Apple2e.rom extends to $C000+)

lc := languageCard.NewCard(rom)
b.Map(0xD000, 0xFFFF, lc)           // line 111: LC card overlays ROM at $D000-$FFFF
b.Map(0xC080, 0xC08F,               // line 112: LC switches overlay I/O at $C080-$C08F
      languageCard.NewSwitches(lc))
```

The ROM remains mapped underneath the LC card (line 98 is not removed). The LC wins the $D000-$FFFF decode because it is mapped last. The ROM device is still present in the bus mapping list and reachable via the `ROMReader` pointer inside the LC -- but it is never reached via bus fallthrough, because the LC itself is the terminal handler for that range. This pattern is described in `03.walkthrough_memory.md` Section 4 (Typical wiring order).

Why map the LC switches *after* I/O (`sw`)? The $C080-$C08F range falls inside the $C000-$C0FF window that `sw` covers. If `sw` were mapped last, it would win the decode for $C080-$C08F and the LC softswitch handler would never fire. Mapping the LC switches after `sw` ensures the LC wins those 16 addresses.

### Boot Trace: Reset Vector Read

At power-on, the 6502 reads the reset vector from $FFFC/$FFFD. Tracing that read through the LC:

1. CPU requests `Read($FFFC)`.
2. Bus walks its mapping list in reverse (last-mapped first). The LC is registered at $D000-$FFFF, which includes $FFFC. The LC wins.
3. `LC.Read($FFFC)` runs with `ramRead=false` (reset state).
4. `c.ramRead` is false, so the card calls `c.rom.Read($FFFC)`.
5. `memory.ROM.Read` computes `offset = $FFFC - rom.Base` and returns the low byte of the reset vector.
6. No LC state bit changes. No bank flip. The machine boots as if no card were present.

This is why the LC can be installed unconditionally (line 111) without conditional logic: its power-on defaults make it a transparent pass-through.

### A Non-Trivial Sequence: Enable Writes to Bank 1, Write, Read Back

The following sequence illustrates the most common "enable LC RAM" prologue used by real Apple II software:

```text
  LDA $C089   ; WRITEBSR1: first read
              ;   bank2Sel = false (bank 1 selected)
              ;   ramRead  = false
              ;   prearm   = true   (first arm read)
              ;   ramWrite = false

  LDA $C089   ; WRITEBSR1: second read (same address, same low & 0x03 == 1)
              ;   bank2Sel = false (bank 1 still)
              ;   ramRead  = false
              ;   prearm was true -> ramWrite = true now
              ;   prearm   = true  (still set after commit)

  STA $E000   ; Write $5A to $E000 with A=$5A
              ;   LC.Write($E000, $5A): ramWrite=true -> c.upper[0] = $5A
              ;   (Note: ramRead is still false -- a read of $E000 now
              ;    returns the ROM byte, not $5A!)

  LDA $C08B   ; RDWRBSR1: first read (low & 0x03 == 3, case 3)
              ;   bank2Sel = false
              ;   ramRead  = true  (case 3 sets ramRead immediately)
              ;   prearm was true -> ramWrite = true again (confirmed)
              ;   prearm   = true
              ;   (Note: prearm was already true from the prior commit,
              ;    so this single read at $C08B both enables RAM reads AND
              ;    re-confirms write-enable in one step -- no second read needed.)

  LDA $E000   ; Now ramRead=true -> ramSlot($E000) -> c.upper[0] = $5A
```

The last read returns `$5A`. Note the gap in the middle: writing `STA $E000` with `ramRead=false` means any *read* of $E000 at that point would still see the ROM byte. `ramRead` and `ramWrite` are independent; enabling one does not enable the other. The final `LDA $C08B` steps up to RDWRBSR1, which sets `ramRead=true` and also confirms `ramWrite` because `prearm` was already set.

## Section 8: Design Decisions and Hardware Reality

**1. Why is `bank2Sel=true` the default at reset instead of `bank1Sel`?**

Historical Apple II+ hardware convention. The power-up state is defined by the card's default jumper wiring, not by any software action. The emulator matches that in `Reset()`. Software that cares about which bank is active picks explicitly via a READBSR1 or READBSR2 strobe. Because `ramRead=false` at reset, the bank-select bit is effectively a no-op at boot -- the card reads ROM regardless of which bank is nominally selected. The default only matters once software enables `ramRead`.

**2. Why does the card hold a direct `ROMReader` pointer instead of going through the bus?**

Recursion prevention. The bus maps the LC as the last device covering $D000-$FFFF, so any bus read in that range goes to the LC. If the LC called the bus for a ROM fallthrough, the bus would route the call back to the LC (last-mapping-wins), causing infinite recursion before the first instruction completed. The `ROMReader` direct pointer bypasses the bus entirely for ROM delegation. See `04.walkthrough_bus.md` Section 4 and the detailed explanation in Section 2 of this walkthrough.

**3. Why is RAM not wiped by `Reset()`?**

Hardware faithfulness. The real Apple Language Card is a static RAM board; the 6502 reset line does not reach the DRAM row address strobes. RAM contents survive a reset press. Some Apple II software relied on this for cold-vs-warm-boot detection: a sentinel byte written to LC RAM before invoking a software reset would still be there afterward, letting the program know it was resuming rather than starting fresh. Clearing RAM in `Reset()` would break those programs.

**4. Why does $C08x read return 0 instead of the floating-bus byte?**

Simplification. On real Apple II+ hardware, a $C08x read returns whatever byte the video DMA scanner most recently placed on the data bus -- the exact value depends on which scanline the video circuit was on at the precise CPU cycle of the read. Emulating that requires a cycle-accurate video-clock model that the current I/O layer does not carry. Returning 0 is a known divergence from real hardware, documented in `languageCard/softswitches.go:14-15`. It is the same class of simplification as `memory.ROM.Read` returning 0xFF for out-of-range addresses (see `03.walkthrough_memory.md` Section 3) -- a harmless constant that prevents crashes without modeling the true open-bus physics.

**5. Why three slices instead of one 16 KB slice?**

Hot-path branch count. A single 16 KB slice would need a two-step decode on every access: first check whether the address falls in the lower 4 KB, and if so, add an offset for the active bank. Three slices let the hot path check `addr >= 0xE000` once; if false, it picks `bank1` or `bank2` directly. See Section 1 and `languageCard/card.go:12-15`. As a secondary benefit, the three-slice layout makes the hardware geometry explicit in the type definition -- a reader who sees `bank1`, `bank2`, and `upper` immediately understands the physical memory organization.

**6. Why does the LC sit on top of the ROM in the bus map instead of replacing it?**

The LC is required to delegate ROM reads when `ramRead=false`. The simplest implementation is to leave the ROM device mapped underneath (providing a valid target for `c.rom.Read`) and layer the LC on top via last-mapping-wins. An alternative -- having the LC own the ROM bytes and removing the ROM device from the bus -- would duplicate `memory.ROM.Read` logic and tie the LC to file-loading concerns. The current design keeps responsibilities separate: `memory.ROM` owns file loading and ROM bytes; `languageCard.Card` owns bank-switching and delegation. See `04.walkthrough_bus.md` Section 4 for last-mapping-wins mechanics.

**7. Why the two-read quirk?**

Historical hardware protection. The write-enable line on the real Apple Language Card required two decode-circuit pulses to close. This was a physical design choice to prevent accidental RAM writes from software that merely queried the LC mode (one read) or wrote a value to a $C08x address (stores). Real-world BASIC interpreters, copy-protection routines, and system initializers all read $C08x addresses as part of normal operation. Requiring two consecutive reads -- a pattern no unintentional code produces -- means those incidental accesses cannot accidentally enable writes and corrupt LC RAM.

**8. Why does `Switches.Write` ignore its value parameter?**

The Apple II+ softswitches are address-strobed, not data-latched. The CPU's `STA $C080` instruction raises an address-decode line; the data byte the CPU places on the bus during that cycle is not routed to any latch inside the softswitch circuit. `Write(addr uint16, _ uint8)` names the value parameter `_` to make this explicit: there is nothing to do with it. See `05.walkthrough_io.md` for the general softswitch pattern. This is the same reason `io.SoftSwitches` ignores write data for its own switches.

## Appendix A: What Flips the Softswitch? -- End-to-End Call Chain

A common confusion when reading `handleSwitch` is not about the prearm logic itself, but about **what actually causes the function to run, and where the `isWrite` flag comes from**. Sections 4 and 6 describe the softswitch in isolation; this appendix traces the full path from a 6502 instruction down to a state change inside the `Card`.

### The Mental Model

The CPU does not know $C089 is special. It just executes generic memory accesses. **Whether a bus access is a "read" or a "write" is decided by the CPU instruction itself, not by the softswitch.** The softswitch is a passive listener -- it gets poked, and reacts to *how* it was poked.

```text
opLDA  -> c.read(addr)  -> bus.Read(addr)  -> Switches.Read  -> handleSwitch(low, isWrite=false)
opSTA  -> c.write(addr) -> bus.Write(addr) -> Switches.Write -> handleSwitch(low, isWrite=true)
```

`isWrite` is literally just "did the CPU execute a load-family or a store-family instruction?"

### End-to-End Trace: `LDA $C089`

The 6502 sees opcode bytes `$AD $89 $C0`. Here is what happens, layer by layer.

**Step 1 -- CPU side** (`cpu/opcodes.go:253`)

```go
func opLDA(c *CPU, mode addrMode) {
    c.A = c.read(c.resolve(mode))   // resolve gives $C089, then c.read fires a bus read
    c.setZN(c.A)
}
```

The CPU just wanted "the byte at $C089." It does not care that $C089 is magic.

**Step 2 -- Bus side**

The bus walks its mapping list in reverse (last-mapping-wins). The LC `Switches` shim is mapped at $C080-$C08F (after `io.SoftSwitches`), so it wins. The bus calls `Switches.Read($C089)`.

**Step 3 -- Shim side** (`languageCard/softswitches.go:16-19`)

```go
func (s *Switches) Read(addr uint16) uint8 {
    s.c.handleSwitch(uint8(addr&0x0F), false /*isWrite*/)
    return 0
}
```

**The shim hardcodes `isWrite=false` in its `Read` method.** That is the only place the flag is born. The byte returned (0) flows back up the chain and lands in register A -- and gets thrown away by whatever code follows.

**Step 4 -- `handleSwitch` fires.** Bank select bit applied, case 1 selected (`low & 3 == 1`), prearm dance steps.

### End-to-End Trace: `STA $C089`

The 6502 sees `$8D $89 $C0`.

**Step 1 -- CPU** (`cpu/opcodes.go:268`)

```go
func opSTA(c *CPU, mode addrMode) { c.write(c.resolve(mode), c.A) }
```

The CPU just wanted "put A's byte at $C089." It does not care that it is magic. The byte written is *whatever happens to be in A* -- almost always irrelevant garbage from earlier code.

**Step 2 -- Bus** routes to `Switches.Write($C089, <whatever>)`.

**Step 3 -- Shim** (`languageCard/softswitches.go:24-26`)

```go
func (s *Switches) Write(addr uint16, _ uint8) {
    s.c.handleSwitch(uint8(addr&0x0F), true /*isWrite*/)
}
```

**The shim hardcodes `isWrite=true` in its `Write` method.** The byte the CPU wrote is `_` -- explicitly thrown away.

**Step 4 -- `handleSwitch` fires.** Same case 1, but `isWrite=true` so the write-arm short-circuit hits: `ramWrite=false`, `prearm=false`.

### How This Explains the Two-Read Quirk

`isWrite` is not a flag software picks. It is the *natural consequence of which 6502 instruction* the programmer wrote at that PC:

| Instructions that hit $C089 with `isWrite=false` | Instructions that hit $C089 with `isWrite=true` |
|---|---|
| `LDA $C089`, `LDX $C089`, `LDY $C089`             | `STA $C089`, `STX $C089`, `STY $C089`            |
| `BIT $C089`, `CMP $C089`, `ADC $C089`, etc.       | (no other write-family addressing modes)         |

So **"arm the write-enable"** = "execute a load-family instruction targeting $C089."
**"Disarm the write-enable"** = "execute a store-family instruction targeting $C089."

The two-read commit pattern means the programmer must literally write:

```asm
LDA $C089       ; first load -> bus read -> isWrite=false -> arms prearm
LDA $C089       ; second load -> bus read -> isWrite=false -> commits ramWrite
```

A stray `STA $C089` anywhere in real code (for example, a copy loop that happens to walk through that address) produces `isWrite=true` and *resets* the dance instead of advancing it. That is the entire safety story.

### Diagram A1: Full Call Chain by Opcode

```text
  CPU opcode byte   |  Op handler  |  Bus method        |  Shim method     |  handleSwitch is called with
  ------------------+--------------+--------------------+------------------+----------------------------
  $AD (LDA abs)     |  opLDA       |  c.read -> Read    |  Switches.Read   |  isWrite=false
  $AE (LDX abs)     |  opLDX       |  c.read -> Read    |  Switches.Read   |  isWrite=false
  $2C (BIT abs)     |  opBIT       |  c.read -> Read    |  Switches.Read   |  isWrite=false
  $8D (STA abs)     |  opSTA       |  c.write -> Write  |  Switches.Write  |  isWrite=true
  $8E (STX abs)     |  opSTX       |  c.write -> Write  |  Switches.Write  |  isWrite=true
```

### The One-Line Answer

The softswitch flips because **the CPU executed some instruction whose effective address landed in $C080-$C08F**. The shim sees the bus knock, classifies it as `Read` or `Write` based on which shim method the bus called, and `handleSwitch` reacts. The byte going across the bus in either direction is irrelevant -- only the *act of accessing the address* matters. This is what "address-strobed, not data-latched" means in Section 6.

## Appendix B: If the LC Overlays the ROM, How Does the CPU Boot from ROM?

Section 7 covers the boot trace in passing, but the question deserves its own walk-through because it confuses readers more often than any other part of the LC design. If the LC is mapped *after* the ROM at $D000-$FFFF -- and last-mapping-wins means the LC wins every decode in that range -- how is the CPU able to read the reset vector at $FFFC/$FFFD from ROM at all?

### The Setup at Install Time

`main.go:96-112` maps the devices in this order:

```text
  b.Map($0000, $BFFF, ram)            // 1st: RAM
  b.Map($C000, $C0FF, sw)             // 2nd: I/O softswitches
  b.Map($D000, $FFFF, rom)            // 3rd: ROM at $D000-$FFFF
  b.Map($D000, $FFFF, lc)             // 4th: LC card OVERLAYS ROM
  b.Map($C080, $C08F, lcSwitches)     // 5th: LC switches overlay I/O
```

After this sequence, the bus's last-mapping-wins rule means **every** read of $D000-$FFFF is routed to `lc`, not to `rom`. The ROM device is still registered in the bus mapping list, but the bus never reaches it via normal address decode.

### The Trick: The LC Is a Transparent Pass-Through at Reset

The LC chooses to be invisible until software activates it. At power-on, `Card.Reset()` (`languageCard/card.go:52-57`) sets:

```go
c.ramRead  = false   // the key bit
c.ramWrite = false
c.bank2Sel = true
c.prearm   = false
```

And `Card.Read` (`languageCard/card.go:61-66`) is:

```go
func (c *Card) Read(addr uint16) uint8 {
    if c.ramRead {
        return c.ramSlot(addr)
    }
    return c.rom.Read(addr)   // delegate straight to ROM
}
```

With `ramRead == false`, every read just forwards to `c.rom.Read(addr)`. The LC is mapped on top, but it **chooses to be invisible** until software flips `ramRead` true.

### The Reset-Vector Trace

The 6502 always starts by reading two bytes from $FFFC/$FFFD to get the address of the first instruction (the "reset vector"). Here is the exact path for `Read($FFFC)`:

```text
  1. CPU pulls Read($FFFC) onto the bus.

  2. Bus walks its mappings backwards (last-mapping-wins):
       - lcSwitches  at $C080-$C08F  -> out of range
       - lc          at $D000-$FFFF  -> HIT, stop.
     Bus calls lc.Read($FFFC).

  3. Card.Read($FFFC):
       c.ramRead is false (reset state)
       -> return c.rom.Read($FFFC)         (direct method call, NOT a bus call)

  4. memory.ROM.Read($FFFC):
       offset = $FFFC - rom.Base
       return romBytes[offset]             (e.g., low byte of reset vector)

  5. The byte flows back: rom -> lc -> bus -> CPU.
     CPU stores it as the low byte of the new PC.
```

Then the same dance for `$FFFD`, and the CPU jumps to whatever address those two bytes form. From there, every instruction fetch from $D000-$FFFF goes through the exact same path: bus -> LC -> (ramRead=false) -> `c.rom.Read`.

### Diagram B1: Boot-time read of $FFFC

```text
                      Bus mapping list (last-mapping-wins)
                      +------------------------------------+
   CPU reads $FFFC    |  ram         $0000-$BFFF           |
        |             |  sw          $C000-$C0FF           |
        |             |  rom         $D000-$FFFF  <- skipped (LC wins)
        +------------>|  lc          $D000-$FFFF  <- HIT
                      |  lcSwitches  $C080-$C08F           |
                      +------------------------------------+
                                    |
                                    v
                      lc.Read($FFFC)
                                    |
                         ramRead == false?
                            |            |
                           yes           no  (at reset: this branch)
                            |            |
                            v            v
                       LC RAM       c.rom.Read($FFFC)   <- direct pointer,
                                             |             bypasses the bus
                                             v
                                      memory.ROM bytes
```

### Why the Direct `c.rom` Pointer Matters

Notice step 3 calls `c.rom.Read(addr)` -- **not** the bus. This is critical. If the LC tried to "fall through" by asking the bus for the same address again, the bus would route the call back to the LC (last-mapping-wins again), and you would get infinite recursion before the CPU even finished its first fetch.

The `ROMReader` pointer stored inside the `Card` (`languageCard/card.go:32`) is the back-channel that lets the LC reach the ROM device directly, skipping the bus and breaking that potential loop. This is also why `rom` must be non-nil at construction -- there is no defensive nil-check in the hot path. See Section 2 ("The `Card` Struct") for the design rationale and Design Decision #2 in Section 8 for the recursion-prevention argument.

### The One-Line Summary

The LC sits on top of the ROM in the bus map, but at reset it is in **proxy mode**: every read forwards to the underlying ROM via a direct pointer. The CPU "starts from ROM" in the observable sense even though every byte technically passes through the LC's `Read` method first. Once software writes the right sequence to $C080-$C08F to set `ramRead = true`, the proxy stops forwarding and starts serving LC RAM instead -- and only then does the ROM become unreachable from the CPU's point of view.
